## What this shows

Each colored curve is one **trajectory** of the Lorenz system, a point moving
through three-dimensional space according to fixed rules. All trajectories
start inside a tiny cube, so at first they move as a single bundle. Within a
few seconds the bundle stretches, folds and breaks apart, and the trajectories
spread over the two "wings" of the **Lorenz attractor**.

Nothing here is random once the run starts: the rules are deterministic. The
seed only chooses the starting points inside the cube. The spreading is
**sensitive dependence on initial conditions**, the hallmark of chaos.

Drag the view to rotate it. On a touch screen, drag sideways to rotate and
swipe up or down to scroll the page.

## How the visualization works

- The equations are integrated with the classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta method
  at a fixed time step of 0.005, so frame rate never changes the result.
- The integrator uses only addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.
  JavaScript requires each of these to be rounded the same way in every browser,
  so a shared link replays exactly, digit for digit, even though the system is
  chaotic.
- Any numerical method makes tiny errors, and chaos amplifies them just as it
  amplifies differences in starting points. What you see is the exact result of
  this method and step size. The computed trajectories still trace out the
  attractor's familiar shape.
- Trails show each trajectory's recent path and fade with age. At high speeds
  several steps happen between frames, and a trail segment then joins the
  sampled positions.

## Parameters

- **Trajectories**: how many points to follow. Restarts the run.
- **Initial spread**: the side length of the starting cube. Smaller cubes keep
  the bundle together for longer. Restarts the run.
- **ρ (rho)**: the system's main parameter (see below). Restarts the run.
- **Speed**: integration steps per second. Applies immediately.

## Mathematical background

Edward Lorenz derived the system in 1963 as a drastically simplified model of
convection in a layer of fluid heated from below:

x′ = σ(y − x), y′ = x(ρ − z) − y, z′ = xy − βz.

Here σ = 10 and β = 8/3 are fixed, as in Lorenz's paper, and ρ (proportional to
the Rayleigh number) is adjustable.

- For ρ < 1, the origin is the only equilibrium and every trajectory decays to
  it.
- For ρ > 1, two more equilibria appear at
  (±√(β(ρ − 1)), ±√(β(ρ − 1)), ρ − 1), the centers of the two wings.
- These equilibria lose stability at ρ = σ(σ + β + 3) / (σ − β − 1), about 24.74
  for these values of σ and β. At Lorenz's value ρ = 28, trajectories never
  settle down and never exactly repeat.

Two trajectories that start close together separate roughly exponentially, until
their separation is as large as the attractor itself. After that, knowing where
one trajectory is tells you almost nothing about the other, even though both
follow the same equations. Applying this to the atmosphere, Lorenz concluded that
predicting the sufficiently distant future is impossible by any method unless
the present conditions are known exactly.

Whether the shape that trajectories trace out is truly a "strange attractor" was
a famous open question, listed by Stephen Smale among his problems for the 21st
century. Warwick Tucker settled it in 2002 with a computer-assisted proof.
