## What this shows

Each square is a **spin**, a tiny magnet that points either up or down, shown
in two contrasting colors (orange and blue in the Classic palette). Neighboring spins lower their energy by pointing the same way, while
temperature flips them at random. Which effect wins depends on the
**temperature** $T$:

- Below the critical temperature $T_c \approx 2.269$, alignment wins.
  Regions of one color grow, and given enough time one color takes over: the
  grid is **magnetized**.
- Above $T_c$, the random flips win. Small patches appear and vanish,
  and on average half the spins point each way.
- Near $T_c$, patches of every size appear at once, from single spins
  to regions nearly as large as the grid.

The corner shows the temperature and the **magnetization per spin** $m$, the
average of all spins (+1 for up, −1 for down).

Things to try: start just above $T_c$ (the default), then drag the
temperature down to 1.5 and watch the regions grow and their boundaries
straighten. Or start with every spin up and raise the temperature past
$T_c$.

## How the visualization works

- Spins are updated with the **Metropolis algorithm**. Each attempt picks a
  spin at random and works out how much flipping it would change the energy,
  $\Delta E$. If the energy would not go up, the spin flips. Otherwise it flips with
  probability $e^{-\Delta E/T}$, so energy-raising flips are rare when
  it's cold and common when it's hot.
- One **sweep** is as many attempts as there are spins, so each spin gets one
  attempt on average.
- The grid wraps around: spins on the right edge are neighbors of spins on the
  left edge, and the same for top and bottom. No spin sits on a boundary.
- All randomness comes from a seeded pseudo-random generator. The two flip
  probabilities are computed with only addition, subtraction, multiplication
  and division, which every browser must round the same way (the built-in
  exponential function has no such guarantee). A shared link therefore replays
  exactly.
- The simulation runs in a Web Worker. The largest grid has 262,144 spins, so
  a single sweep is far too much work for the page's own thread at high speed.
  If a device can't keep up, the simulation runs slower than requested instead
  of freezing the page.

## Parameters

- **Grid size**: spins along each side. Restarts the run.
- **Temperature**: applies immediately, from the next sweep. A shared link
  replays the run from the start at the temperature in the link, so changes
  made during a run are not part of it.
- **Starting spins**: random (as if infinitely hot) or all up (as if at
  absolute zero). Restarts the run.
- **Speed**: sweeps per second. Applies immediately and never changes the
  results.

## Mathematical background

A configuration assigns $s_i = +1$ or $-1$ to every site $i$. Its
energy is

$$
H = -\sum_{\langle i, j \rangle} s_i s_j,
$$

summed over all pairs of neighboring sites. Each aligned pair contributes −1
and each opposed pair +1. At temperature $T$, a configuration occurs with
probability proportional to $e^{-H/T}$ (the **Boltzmann
distribution**). At low temperatures the low-energy, aligned configurations
dominate; at high temperatures every configuration is almost equally likely.
The Metropolis algorithm is a Markov chain whose long-run distribution is
exactly this one, which is why it is used to sample it.

Ernst Ising solved the one-dimensional chain in 1925 and found no phase
transition at any positive temperature. In two dimensions there is one. Lars
Onsager solved the square-lattice model exactly in 1944, with the transition at

$$
T_c = \frac{2}{\ln\bigl(1 + \sqrt{2}\bigr)} \approx 2.269.
$$

C. N. Yang later derived the magnetization below $T_c$:

$$
m = \bigl(1 - \sinh^{-4}(2/T)\bigr)^{1/8},
$$

about 0.986 at $T = 1.5$ and 0.911 at $T = 2$. It falls to zero at
$T_c$ very steeply, and stays zero above it.

A finite grid differs from these infinite-lattice results in a few ways you
can see:

- Up and down are symmetric, so which color wins from a random start is a
  matter of chance.
- Starting from random spins below $T_c$, the regions grow slowly,
  roughly like the square root of the number of sweeps. Stripes that wrap all
  the way around the grid can last a very long time.
- Near $T_c$, large regions take many sweeps to change. This
  **critical slowing down** is a property of single-spin algorithms like
  Metropolis, not of the model itself.
